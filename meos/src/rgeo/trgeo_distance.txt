Temporal Distance for Rigid Geometries
======================================

This file describes the algorithm that gives the temporal distance between
a temporal rigid geometry and a geometry, a temporal point or another
temporal rigid geometry.

The algorithm follows the paper of Schoemans, Sakr and Zimanyi [1].  It
follows the pair of closest features (vertices or edges) of the two bodies,
and it gets the distance from this pair.  This file adds three things to
the paper:

  * All the equations of the algorithm are functions of one small family.
    Thus one solver finds the changes of feature, the extrema of the
    distance and the times of contact.

  * The solver finds all the roots of a function, with a proof for each
    part of the interval.  The paper uses a numerical method and assumes
    that the method finds all the roots.

  * The two bodies can overlap.  Then the distance is zero.  The paper
    does not handle this case.

The walk over the closest features and the complexity results of the paper
do not change.

Motion
------

A rigid geometry is a reference polygon and a temporal pose.  In a
segment of the temporal pose, the time t goes from 0 to 1, and the pose
changes linearly.  A point a of body A, relative to the rotation center of
A, moves as

    vA(t) = R(thA + wA*t) * a + cA + t*dA

R(x) is the rotation by the angle x, cA is the rotation center at t = 0,
dA is the displacement of the center, and wA is the rotation in the
segment.  This is Equation (5) of the paper.  The body is rigid, thus the
lengths and directions of its edges and the radii of its vertices are
constants.

The notation is:

    u     = a_e - a_s     an edge of A, relative to the rotation center
    L     = |u|           the length of the edge
    beta  = angle(u)      the direction of the edge
    b                     a vertex of B, relative to the rotation center of B
    rho   = |b|           the radius of the vertex
    alpha = angle(b)      the angle of the vertex
    W(t)  = (cB - cA) + t*(dB - dA)     the offset between the two centers

W(t) contains the two translations, and it is linear in t.

The rotation in a segment takes the shorter way from the first angle to the
second, and a very small rotation is zero, as in the interpolation of the
poses.  Thus the algorithm places a body at the same position as the
database.

The Function Family
-------------------

Equation (8) of the paper gives the projection parameter s of a point p on
an edge from vs to ve.  Its numerator is

    N(t) = dot( p(t) - vs(t), ve(t) - vs(t) )

         = L * [ Wx(t)*cos(thA + wA*t + beta) + Wy(t)*sin(thA + wA*t + beta) ]
         + rho * L * cos( (wA - wB)*t + thA - thB + beta - alpha )
         - dot(a_s, u)

The cross product gives the signed distance from p to the line of the
edge:

    G(t) = cross( p(t) - vs(t), ve(t) - vs(t) )

         = L * [ Wx(t)*sin(thA + wA*t + beta) - Wy(t)*cos(thA + wA*t + beta) ]
         + rho * L * sin( (wA - wB)*t + thA - thB + beta - alpha )
         - cross(a_s, u)

The paper says that s(t) is not linear and uses a numerical method.  The
expansion shows its form.  N and G are both functions of the family

    F(t) = SUM_k [ (a1_k*t + a0_k)*cos(w_k*t) + (b1_k*t + b0_k)*sin(w_k*t) ]
         + c2*t^2 + c1*t + c0

In one segment, there are at most three frequencies: wA, wB and wA - wB.
The squared distance between two moving vertices is also in the family.
The derivative of a function of the family is in the family:

    d/dt [ (a1*t + a0)*cos(w*t) + (b1*t + b0)*sin(w*t) ]
      = (w*b1*t + a1 + w*b0)*cos(w*t) + (-w*a1*t + b1 - w*a0)*sin(w*t)

Thus the derivative of a distance function comes from its coefficients,
without sampling, and an extremum is a root of a function of the family.

Two facts make the equations simpler:

  * s = 0 is N = 0, and s = 1 is N = L^2.  Thus the two tests of Equations
    (12) and (17) of the paper are one function at two levels.

  * While 0 < s < 1, the distance from p to the edge is |G| / L.  Thus the
    extrema of the distance to an edge are the roots of G', and the roots
    of G are the contacts.  The paper does not give this derivation.

Parallel Edges
--------------

Equations (18) and (19) of the paper test if an edge of A and an edge of B
are parallel, and the paper solves them with a numerical method.  A
rotation does not change a cross product, thus

    P(t) = cross( uA(t), uB(t) )
         = LA * LB * sin( (wB - wA)*t + thB - thA + betaB - betaA )

P is a sinusoid of constant amplitude, and its roots have a closed form.
If wA = wB, P is constant: the two edges are parallel during the whole
segment or never.  This is the special case of Section 4.3.3 of the paper.

Root Isolation
--------------

The solver must find all the roots of a function of the family on an
interval.  A method that samples the function and looks for changes of
sign does not do this.  It misses two roots between two samples, and it
misses an extremum in the first or the last cell.

The solver splits the interval into cells.  For each cell, it does one of
these steps:

  * If F changes sign in the cell, it refines the root with Newton's method
    and bisection.

  * If |F(m)| > (h/2) * max|F'|, with m the middle and h the width of the
    cell, the cell has no root.  No point of the cell is farther than h/2
    from m, thus F cannot reach zero.  The bound of |F'| comes from the
    coefficients: a sine or a cosine is at most one, and an affine
    coefficient has its extremes at the ends of the cell.

  * If |F'(m)| > (h/2) * max|F''|, F is monotonic in the cell.  F does not
    change sign in the cell, thus the cell has no root.

  * Else, it splits the cell in two.

Thus each cell gives a root, or a proof that it has no root.  The proof
makes the result correct, not the number of cells.

The first split uses cells of about a quarter turn of phase, which makes
the proofs succeed with few splits.  Write one term as

    L * [ Wx*cos(w*t + g) + Wy*sin(w*t + g) ]
      = L * |W(t)| * cos(w*t + g - delta(t))

with delta(t) = angle(W(t)).  W(t) moves on a line, thus |W|^2 is a
convex quadratic and delta changes by less than pi.  Thus the phase of the
terms changes by at most |w1| + |w2| + |w3| + pi.  A rotation takes the
shorter way, thus |wA| and |wB| are at most pi, and the first split has at
most 20 cells.

In tests with random functions, a function had at most four roots in
[0, 1].

A touch of zero needs care.  Near a touch, F stays within the tolerance on
a small interval, and each cell boundary in that interval is a root.  Thus
a touch can give tens of roots.  Of a group of roots between which F stays
within the tolerance, the solver keeps the first and the last.  For the
crossings, the group is one root, see below, thus the two are sufficient.

Crossings
.........

The walk needs the crossings of a level, not all the roots.  A touch of a
boundary does not change the region.  The root at the time of the current
event is the boundary that the walk just crossed.

The solver finds all the roots, thus F has a constant sign between two
consecutive roots.  The sign in the middle of each gap between two roots
gives the sign in the whole gap.  A root is a crossing if the gaps before
and after it have opposite signs.  The direction of the crossing is the
sign after it.  A root at the start of the interval has no gap before it,
thus it is not a crossing.  A touch has the same sign on the two sides,
thus it is not a crossing.  A gap in which F stays within the tolerance
has no sign, and a group of roots with such gaps between them counts as
one root.

The sign comes from values of F.  It does not come from F' at the root,
because F' is zero at a touch and its sign is not reliable near a touch.

Side after a time
.................

The walk must know on which side of a boundary the origin is just after
the time t of an event.  At an event, the origin is often exactly on a
boundary, thus the value alone cannot decide.  The side is the sign of the
first term of the Taylor series at t that is not zero: the value, then
F', then F''.  A crossing gets the sign of F', and a touch gets the sign
of F''.  If the three terms are zero, the origin moves along the boundary,
and a tie rule decides, see Ties.

The Walk
--------

The distance between A and B is the distance from the origin to their
Minkowski difference A - B.  If A and B are convex, A - B is a convex
polygon.  Its edges, in counterclockwise order, are the edges of A and the
reversed edges of B, sorted by direction.  Each edge of A goes with the
vertex of B that supports it.  Each edge of B goes with the vertex of A that
supports it.  Thus the features of A - B are pairs of features of A and B:

    VV(i,j)  the vertex a_i - b_j
    EV(i,j)  the edge from a_i - b_j to a_{i+1} - b_j
    VE(i,j)  the edge from a_i - b_j to a_i - b_{j+1}

These are the vertex-vertex, edge-vertex and vertex-edge pairs of Section
4.3 of the paper.  The walk follows the feature of A - B that is closest
to the origin.  Each quantity of the walk is a function of the family:

    EV(i,j)   N and G of the vertex b_j and the edge i of A
    VE(i,j)   N and G of the vertex a_i and the edge j of B
    VV(i,j)   the squared distance between a_i and b_j

For VE(i,j), N goes in the same direction along the reversed edge.

If B is a point, it has one vertex and no edges, and A - B is A moved by
-b.  Then the walk is the walk of a point and a polygon in the paper.

If B is a segment, it is a convex polygon with two vertices and two edges
in opposite directions.  The outside of each edge is the inside of the
other edge, thus the body has no inside.  The walk needs no other change
for a segment.

Order of the edges
..................

The order of the edges of A - B changes when an edge of A and an edge of B
become parallel.  Edge i of A comes before the reversed edge j of B if the
angle from the first edge to the second edge is in [0, pi).

  * While the angle is not 0 or pi, the sign of P gives the order.  The
    cross product of edge i of A and the reversed edge j of B is -P.

  * The roots of P give the times at which the order changes.

  * If the two edges stay parallel, the sign of the cosine of the angle
    separates 0 from pi.

For a polygon B, the angle pi has no effect: the two reversed edges at a
vertex of B are at less than a half turn from each other.  For a segment,
they are at exactly a half turn.  Without the cosine, an edge of A that is
parallel to the segment comes before the two reversed edges of the segment.

Thus:

  * EV(i,j) is an edge of A - B if edge i of A is between the reversed
    edges j - 1 and j of B.  When edge i of A passes the reversed edge j,
    the vertex of B that supports it changes to b_{j+1}.  For VE(i,j), the
    same is true with A and B exchanged.

  * The edges before and after the vertex VV(i,j) are the first and the last
    of two candidates in this order.

Events
......

The feature changes at these events:

  * EV or VE: N crosses 0 down (to the start vertex) or L^2 up (to the end
    vertex).  P crosses 0, which moves the supporting vertex.

  * VV: N of the next edge crosses 0 up, or N of the previous edge crosses
    L^2 down.  P crosses 0 for the edges around the vertex, which changes
    the next or the previous edge but not the feature.

In the paper, the walk passes through an edge-edge pair at these times.
Here, an edge-edge pair is two edges of A - B on the same line, and the
walk leaves it in the same step.

While a feature does not change, the distance is one function of the
family, and its extrema are the crossings of the derivative.

At each event, the walk checks again that the origin is in the region of
its feature, and it moves to a neighbor feature if the origin is not.
Two events come from two different functions.  In a thin region, their
times can differ by a rounding error, and the origin can already be past
the next boundary when the walk arrives in the region.

If the walk has no feature yet, or if the origin is inside the line of the
edge of its feature, a scan of all the edges of A - B gives the closest
feature, or tells that the origin is inside A - B.  The scan compares
distances only, thus the local check also examines its result.

Ties
....

If the origin moves along a boundary between two regions, the two
features give the same distance.  Then fixed rules decide:

  * Between a vertex and an edge, the walk takes the edge.  The walk finds
    the entry of the origin into A - B on an edge.  Thus it must not stay on
    a vertex through which the origin enters.  This can happen at a vertex
    of A - B between two edges on the same line.  Also, the test to go from
    a vertex to an edge is the exact opposite of the test to go back, thus
    the walk cannot go back and forth.

  * Between an edge of A and a reversed edge of B in the same direction,
    the edge of A comes first.  Any fixed rule is correct, because the two
    edges are then on the same line of A - B.  It is necessary that each
    question gets the same answer.

  * A point on the line of an edge is outside A - B.  Its distance is zero
    for the two choices.

Thresholds
..........

The walk can also give the times at which the distance crosses a
threshold d.  For a vertex, the squared distance crosses d^2.  For an
edge, G crosses d * L.  At these times, the distance is exactly d.

Overlap
-------

Section 4.2 of the paper says that the algorithm fails if the bodies
intersect.  V-Clip [2] is defined for disjoint bodies.  When the bodies
overlap, there is no closest feature.  Here, an overlap is a state in which
the distance is zero, and the walk finds its start and its end.

  * Entry: while the feature is an edge, G crosses 0 down.  This also finds
    an entry through a vertex, because the tie rule puts the walk on an
    edge.  An entry at the time of an event is a crossing at the start of
    the interval, which is not a crossing.  Then the origin is inside the
    line of the edge, and the scan finds the entry.

  * Exit: the origin leaves A - B through an edge.  Thus the exit is the
    first crossing up of G of an edge feature, at a time at which the
    feature is an edge of A - B and the origin projects on it.  The walk
    examines each edge feature, which costs one root isolation for each of
    the 2*n*m features.  The edge of the exit is the feature of the walk
    after the exit.

A faster exit is possible.  The signed separation

    sigma(t) = max over the edge normals of A and B of the separation along
               the normal

is the distance when the bodies are disjoint, and minus the penetration
depth when they overlap.  The normal that gives the maximum is a witness
of the overlap, as the closest feature is a witness of the distance.  The
walk can follow this normal from one segment to the next.  For an edge of
A with unit normal n0, the separation along n0 is

    gap(t) = dot( R(thA + wA*t)*n0, W(t) )
           + min over the vertices b of B of
               dot( R((thA - thB) + (wA - wB)*t)*n0, b )
           - hA(n0)

The first term is in the family.  The second term is the support function
of B along a direction that turns at a constant rate.  It is a sinusoid
of constant amplitude between two changes of the supporting vertex, and
the changes have a closed form.  hA(n0) is a constant of A.  This is not
implemented, because the simple exit is correct and costs time only while
the bodies overlap.

Non-Convex Bodies
-----------------

The walk needs convex bodies.  Section 4.4 of the paper splits a
non-convex body into convex parts, computes the N*M distances between the
parts, and merges them.  This design does not include that step.

If the split into convex parts is added, two points can differ from the
paper:

  * A bounding volume hierarchy of the edges, relative to the rotation
    center, removes most pairs of parts before the walk.  It is built once
    for each reference geometry.

  * The merge compares the distances of the parts with their exact values
    at the instants, not with their linear interpolations only.

Code
----

The root finder
...............

meos/src/rgeo/trgeo_distsolve.c contains the function family and the root
isolation.  It includes no PostgreSQL, PostGIS or MEOS header and does no
allocation, so that it builds on its own, without libmeos.  Thus a test
program can check the same code that MEOS uses, on many random functions.
Do not add palloc, Assert or meos_error to this file: they need MEOS, and
Assert and meos_error can jump out of a function, which changes the
control flow between the two builds.

The caller gives the arrays for the roots.  An array of DISTFUN_MAXROOTS
roots is sufficient in practice.  A function with more roots gives the
result DISTFUN_OVERFLOW, not a wrong answer.  The caller also gives the
tolerance, because only the caller knows the size of the geometry.

The solver must terminate.  Two roots closer than (hi - lo) * 1e-14 are
the same root, and the number of cells has a limit.  A function that needs
more cells gives the result DISTFUN_BUDGET.

The test program meos/test/unit/rgeo/trgeo_distsolve_unit.c checks that the
solver finds each change of sign of a dense scan, that each root is a
root, that the crossings and their directions agree with the scan, and
that a touch is not a crossing.  CMake builds it in a MEOS build with rigid
geometries, and ctest runs it with a small number of trials.  To run it by
hand with more trials:

  gcc -O2 -Wall -I meos/include -o trgeo_distsolve_unit \
      meos/test/unit/rgeo/trgeo_distsolve_unit.c \
      meos/src/rgeo/trgeo_distsolve.c -lm
  ./trgeo_distsolve_unit 20000

Relation to the Paper
---------------------

In a revision of the paper, most of this file goes into existing
sections:

  * The expansion of N(t) comes after Equation (8), in Section 4.1.

  * The closed form of Equations (18) and (19) replaces the numerical
    method, in Section 4.3.2.

  * The distance |G|/L and its extrema as the roots of G' go in Section
    6.1, where the paper omits the derivation.

Two parts need their own sections:

  * The overlap state removes a limit of the algorithm, and needs a
    section near Section 4.4.

  * The root isolation is a numerical argument, and goes in an appendix,
    with a reference from Section 4.2.1, where the paper assumes that the
    solution of one equation has a constant cost.
